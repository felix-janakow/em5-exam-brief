Service & Service Systems

Value sits in use, not in the product. This session gives you the lens (service-dominant logic) and the strategy that follows from it (servitization and product-service systems).

1.1

S-D logic vs. G-D logic

Core idea

In G-D logic the firm puts value into a product and the price captures it. In S-D logic value only appears when the customer uses the firm's resources together with their own.

Goods-dominant (G-D) logic is the traditional view. The firm alone creates value through its supply chain, builds it into a product and captures it through the price. The customer is the end of the line who "uses up" the value.

Service-dominant (S-D) logic (Vargo & Lusch) turns this around. The firm can only propose value. Value is created in use, when the customer integrates the firm's resources with their own, so it is always co-created. Goods are still important, but as appliances: vehicles that carry the maker's competences to the customer.

G-D logicS-D logic
ValueValue-in-exchange: built into the product, captured through the priceValue-in-use: created when resources are used in the customer's context
Who creates valueThe firm alone, through its supply chainFirm, partners and customer together (co-creation)
Role of the firmProduce and distribute valuePropose value and co-create it
Role of goodsUnits of output with value built inAppliances (vehicles) for delivering service
Role of the customer"Uses up" or "destroys" valueCo-creates value by integrating the firm's resources with their own
Example

A dishwasher creates value only when it actually washes your dishes. An unused machine in a warehouse has no value yet; the value appears in your kitchen, with your water, power and time.

1.2

Service, service systems, ecosystems and co-creation

Core idea

Value is co-created only in the joint sphere where provider and customer meet. Interaction, access to processes and decision power make that sphere bigger.

Service (singular) is the application of competences (knowledge, skills, resources) for the benefit of another party, in a way both agree on and both gain from. It is a process and the substance of all economic exchange, not a unit of output.

Services are usually described by the "III": intangibility, interactivity (the customer must take part, as with a fitness coach) and individuality. Products and services are not opposites; they sit on one spectrum, with solutions, PSS and hybrid offerings in between.

A service system is a configuration of resources (people, information, technology) linked to other systems by a value proposition and shared information such as language, prices and laws. Think of one provider and one customer. A service ecosystem is a system of service systems: many actors interact many-to-many, adjust themselves and are held together by institutional arrangements, the shared rules and norms. A car maker working with repair shops, navigation and entertainment providers is an ecosystem.

Don't confuse

Service system: provider and customer linked by a value proposition (dyadic). Service ecosystem: many-to-many actors in a context shaped by institutions.

1.3

Servitization and product-service systems

Core idea

Servitization moves a firm from selling products to selling product-service systems. Neely's five types differ in how much service is in the offer and in who owns the product.

Servitization is a change process inside a firm: it shifts from a product-centric to a service-centric business logic and sells product-service systems (PSS). Firms do it to seize market opportunities, to defend markets and customers (differentiation, lock-in) and to leverage technology such as smart products and data.

Servitization targets value-in-use by tapping the customer's application space: where the product is used (location), when (timing), how sliced (lot size, term, transformation, such as single screws instead of boxes) and how individualized (specific needs).

TypeOwnershipWhat the customer buysExamples
1 Integration-orientedCustomerProduct plus downstream services through vertical integrationRetail, financing, logistics; a juice maker opening juice bars
2 Product-orientedCustomerProduct plus services directly related to itDesign, installation, training, consulting
3 Service-orientedCustomerProduct with services built in as an integral partMachine health monitoring in every contract
4 Use-orientedProviderAvailability or usageLeasing, sharing, Philips pay-per-lux, Hilti Fleet Management
5 Result-orientedProvider, or no productAn outcome; the provider chooses the meansCloud storage, Trumpf pay-per-part, price per resolved case

Evidence on manufacturers. Services are common but stay close to the product. About 30% of capital-goods makers offer pay-per-use, yet 74% of their revenue still comes from product sales, and in 2023 less than 1% of equipment sales ran on as-a-service contracts. Firms also withdraw service models again: BMW and Mercedes sold ShareNow in 2022.

Don't confuse
  • S-D logic is a perspective on all value creation and applies to every firm. Servitization is a change process and strategy inside one firm.
  • Service-oriented: the customer owns the product. Use-oriented: the provider keeps ownership. Ask "who owns it?" first.
  • Use-oriented: the customer pays for having and using the tool. Result-oriented: the customer pays for the output (drilled holes, printed parts) and the provider picks the means.

Exam-style tasks

Session 01 · 3 tasks · 7 P
Task 1.12.5 P

Define service in the sense of service-dominant logic (S-D logic). What role does a cordless power drill play from an S-D logic perspective?

Model answer

Definition 1 P. Service (singular) is the application of competences (knowledge, skills, resources) by one party for the benefit of another, in a mutually agreed and mutually beneficial way. It is a process of doing something for someone and the substance of all economic exchange, not a unit of output.

Role of the drill 1 P. Goods are appliances used in service provision. The drill gives the customer access to the maker's competences, such as its engineering know-how, packed into a device, and supports the customer's own activity.

Value 0.5 P. The value is not embedded in the drill and not captured by its price. It arises in use, when the customer integrates the drill with their own resources (skills, time, the wall) and gets the hole for the shelf. Value is co-created as value-in-use.

Task 1.22 P

Hilti offers construction companies "Fleet Management": for a fixed monthly fee per tool, a company gets the power tools it needs. The tools remain Hilti's property; Hilti repairs them, replaces them when needed and swaps them for newer models. According to Neely's PSS framework, classify this offering and explain your decision.

Model answer

Type 1 P: use-oriented PSS.

Justification 0.5 P: Hilti keeps ownership of the tools. The customer pays only for having the tools available and using them, not for the tools themselves; repair and replacement are part of the fee.

Delimitation 0.5 P: despite its name it is not service-oriented, because there ownership passes to the customer (as when a provider sells a vehicle fleet to a haulier and then manages it). It is not result-oriented either, because the company still uses the tools with its own workers and does not buy an output such as finished drill holes.

Task 1.32.5 P

A maker of irrigation pumps for farms wants to "become a service company". Explain the difference between service-dominant logic and servitization, and use the pump maker to illustrate servitization.

Model answer

S-D logic 1 P is a perspective on economic exchange. All exchange is service; goods are appliances used in service provision; value is co-created in use. It applies to every firm, even one that only sells pumps.

Servitization 1 P is a change process inside a firm. The firm innovates its capabilities and processes to move from a product-centric business logic to selling product-service systems.

Difference and illustration 0.5 P: S-D logic is a way of looking at value creation; servitization is a strategy that changes what a firm offers. The pump maker servitizes if it adds remote pump monitoring and maintenance contracts for its installed base, or sells irrigation water per cubic metre instead of pumps. Service science looks at service systems as systems of actors; the manufacturing view looks at PSS as systems of deliverables.

2.1

Innovation terms and types

Core idea

An invention becomes an innovation only when it reaches the market. Henderson & Clark classify innovations by two questions: are the core concepts overturned, and are the links between components changed?

The traditional view puts all innovation on one line from incremental to radical. Henderson & Clark add a second dimension, the linkages between components. That reveals two types the line hides: modular innovation swaps a core concept but keeps the architecture, and architectural innovation keeps the components but connects them in a new way.

2.2

Architectural innovation

Core idea

Architectural innovation combines existing components in a new way. Digital services are ideal for it, yet experts miss it because they see each component only in its current use.

Lecture examples: the 1945 US Naval Observatory table printer (an electric typewriter plus a punch-card reader), Skyscanner as a mashup of booking services, the Starbucks app putting the loyalty card on the phone, and AI agents linking apps via MCP, "the mashups of the 2020s". Kodak's first digital camera (1975) was an architectural innovation that became the precursor to a radical one.

Two barriers stand in the way. Functional fixedness: deep topical expertise creates fixed mental models, so experts see each component only in its current use. Size of the solution space: with about 50 academic disciplines and three needed for one idea, there are 117,600 multidisciplinary knowledge profiles. In-house R&D cannot cover that, so firms open up to outside ideas (Session 03). A third weakness is that a combination of existing parts can hardly be patented, so it is easy to copy.

2.3

Disruptive innovation

Core idea

A disruptive innovation starts simple and cheap in a tier the leaders ignore, then improves until it is good enough for the mainstream, while the incumbent overshoots what customers need.

Christensen defines a disruptive innovation as a technologically simple product, service or business model that takes root in a market tier the leaders find unattractive. Products that match market needs today often overshoot them tomorrow, which leaves room below.

The innovator's dilemma explains why well-run incumbents ignore it. The incumbent does everything right for its best customers and still loses. On top comes the boiling frog effect: change is noticed too late. Kodak; Media Markt, which went online only in 2012, 17 years after amazon.com.

ResponseAssessmentExample
Within the same organizationVery, very difficult; needs ambidextrous managersApple Newton (failure), iPod and iPhone (success)
Acquire a start-upGood; the price depends on market timingDeutsche Bank buying fintechs
New unit at arm's length, grow organicallyOften the best optionHUK-Coburg and HUK24

Don't confuse

Disruptive describes a market path: simpler, cheaper, starts at the low end or with non-customers and changes the market structure. Radical describes technical novelty: core concepts and linkages are overturned. Disruptive offers are often technologically simple.

2.4

Service innovation challenges and ambidexterity

Core idea

Service firms must run today's business efficiently and explore new business at the same time. Without an R&D department to hold exploration, they need structural or contextual ambidexterity.

ExploitationExploration
FocusUse what the firm already knowsDiscover something new
PrizesEfficiency, control, incremental improvementFlexibility, autonomy, experimentation
HorizonShort term, closeLong term, distant
RiskLow uncertainty, high success ratesConsumes resources now, uncertain and delayed returns, many failures

Ambidexterity (O'Reilly & Tushman) is the ability to do both: compete in mature markets where efficiency counts and in new ones where experimentation counts. The organizational challenge is to manage parts with diverging targets, styles, freedoms, processes and cost focus. Product firms can park exploration in R&D; service firms usually can't, which makes it harder.

That links to the seven challenges of service innovation: (1) innovations are mostly externally driven, by competition and market change (55%) or by accident (24%), and only 21% are strategically planned; (2) patents and R&D spend don't fit as metrics, so use the share of revenue from new services; (3) service quality is hard to measure; (4) services are easy to imitate, so protect them through process innovation and customer loyalty; (5) they require organizational change and trigger resistance; (6) they happen in complex ecosystems that need orchestration; (7) the customer plays a central role.

Exam-style tasks

Session 02 · 3 tasks · 10 P
Task 2.13.5 P

A supermarket chain links three things it already has: its loyalty app, its online recipe collection and its click-and-collect service. Customers pick a recipe in the app, all ingredients go into their basket, and they collect the bag at their store two hours later. (1) According to Henderson and Clark, which type of innovation is this? Name the type and explain why it fits. (2) Discuss one potential barrier associated with this type of innovation.

Model answer

(1) Type 2 P: architectural innovation. The core concepts are reinforced: the loyalty app, the recipe collection and click-and-collect already exist and keep working as before. What is new is the linkage between them, from recipe to basket to pickup, much like Starbucks putting its loyalty card on the phone. It is not modular, because no component is replaced by a new concept, and not radical, because the core concepts are not overturned.

(2) Barrier 1.5 P, one of these:

  • Functional fixedness: the experts in each department see their component only in its current use (the app as a coupon tool, recipes as marketing content) and do not think of new combinations.
  • Size of the solution space: architectural innovation combines knowledge from several fields, and there are too many combinations to search with in-house experts alone, which is why firms open up to outside ideas.
  • Imitation: a combination of existing components can hardly be patented, so competitors can copy it quickly.
Task 2.23.5 P

Hearing-aid makers sell high-end devices through audiologists at high margins. Now cheap consumer earbuds with a simple "hearing boost" mode appear in electronics stores. At first they only help people with mild hearing loss who never bought a hearing aid. Describe the innovator's dilemma and discuss its potential impact on an established hearing-aid maker. Propose one strategy to prevent or reduce this risk.

Model answer

Description 1.5 P: successful incumbents can do everything right and still lose leadership to disruptive innovations. A disruptive innovation is technologically simple and takes root in a tier the leaders find unattractive. Incumbents ignore it because their most profitable customers cannot use it at first, it starts in emerging, insignificant markets, and it is simpler and cheaper with lower margins (fear of cannibalization). Running the profitable business and building the new one at the same time is hard.

Impact 1 P: the maker sees the earbuds as irrelevant, since its core customers with severe hearing loss cannot use them and they would eat into high-margin devices and the audiologist channel. As the earbuds improve, they move from mild hearing loss into the core market. Like the boiling frog, the maker notices too late, loses share and margin, and catching up on a missed cycle is tedious if possible at all.

Strategy 1 P, one named and justified: acquire an earbud start-up (price depends on timing); set up a new unit at arm's length with its own brand and budget that sells simple hearing earbuds and grows organically (often the best option); build it within the same organization (very hard, needs ambidextrous managers); or live and let die.

Task 2.33 P

A city utility runs the local power grid with strict safety rules, long planning cycles and a strong focus on efficiency. A small team has built an app-based subscription for charging electric cars, and the pilot is a success. The utility wants to scale the charging service while keeping grid operations safe and efficient. Explain the ambidexterity challenge (exploration vs. exploitation), then describe one concrete tension the utility might face.

Model answer

Ambidexterity 1.5 P: the organization must both exploit and explore. Exploitation uses existing knowledge in daily business; it prizes efficiency, control and incremental improvement, with low uncertainty and high success rates. Exploration looks for new business; it needs flexibility, autonomy and experimentation, consumes resources now and has uncertain, delayed returns. The challenge is to manage, at the same time, parts with diverging targets, styles, freedoms, processes and cost focus. It is harder for service firms, which cannot park exploration in an R&D department.

Tension 1.5 P, one concrete example tied to the case, naming both sides: the grid side works with strict safety approvals and long release cycles, while the charging team needs to change its app and tariffs every few weeks and test them with customers. Or: the same IT staff and control-room engineers are needed for both, and their targets reward uptime and low cost, not experiments that may fail. A way to handle it may be added: a separate unit coordinated by top management (structural) or protected innovation time (contextual).

3.1

Open innovation

Core idea

Open innovation lets knowledge cross the firm boundary on purpose: in from outside, out to others, or both ways with a partner.

Chesbrough & Bogers define open innovation as a distributed innovation process based on purposively managed knowledge flows across organizational boundaries. It combines internal and external ideas and internal and external paths to market. The flows can be pecuniary (licensing) or non-pecuniary (free revealing).

It replaces the closed model, in which a firm discovers, develops and ships everything from its own lab (Bell Labs, IBM Watson Research). That model eroded as knowledge workers became more numerous and mobile, venture capital funded spin-outs, and knowledge spread worldwide.

Closed principleOpen principle
The smart people in the field work for us.Not all the smart people work for us; work with smart people inside and outside.
To profit from R&D we must discover, develop and ship it ourselves.External R&D can create significant value; internal R&D is needed to claim part of it.
If we discover it, we get to market first, and first wins.We don't have to originate the research to profit. A better business model beats getting to market first.
Control our IP so competitors can't profit.Profit from others using our IP; buy others' IP when it advances our model.

Open, but not too open. The paradox of openness (Laursen & Salter): innovating requires sharing knowledge, capturing value requires protecting it. Further tensions: not-invented-here resistance; absorptive capacity, since a firm can only use outside knowledge it can recognize, assimilate and apply, which needs internal R&D; and appropriability, capturing value through patents, secrecy, lead time or complementary assets.

3.2

Wisdom of crowds vs. collective intelligence

Core idea

A wise crowd needs independent judgments that are aggregated. Collective intelligence works the other way round, through interaction and shared knowledge.

Surowiecki: under the right conditions groups are remarkably intelligent and often smarter than their smartest members. Collective intelligence (Malone) is "groups of individuals doing things collectively that seem intelligent"; Woolley showed a measurable c-factor for group performance. The mechanism is different: here people interact and build on each other's knowledge, as in a jointly written repair manual.

3.3

Tools for user and crowd innovation

Core idea

Contests, communities, toolkits and living labs organize the crowd in different ways. The CI Genome classifies any of them by four questions: what, who, why and how.

3.4

Innovation partnerships (B2B)

Core idea

Cooperate where you don't differentiate, compete on top. Firms share spaces, start-ups, data and standards with others to grow the pie.

Innovation campsites
Co-location spaces for incumbents and start-ups with shared facilities, where unplanned encounters spark ideas (Philips High Tech Campus).
Start-up ecosystems
Incubators mentor start-ups. Platform ecosystems: start-ups build apps on the corporate's platform (SAP). Venture client: the corporate buys the start-up's product (BMW). Corporate venture capital: the corporate buys a stake.
Consortia & foundations
Competitors jointly define a standard (Matter, 3GPP for 5G) or co-develop non-differentiating software in a neutral foundation (Eclipse SDV, Linux Foundation), then compete on top.
Example

Tesla's charging connector. Tesla opened its connector as NACS in 2022. Ford, GM and others switched, SAE standardized it as J3400, and in 2026 Superchargers are open to all major brands. Tesla won the standards war, network effects and charging revenue from competitors' customers.

Don't confuse

Open data: anyone, any purpose, users mostly unknown. Data sharing / data spaces: selected partners under usage policies.

3.5

Open source and GenAI reshape open innovation

Core idea

GenAI beats the crowd on average quality, speed and cost. Humans keep the edge at the extremes: top novelty, diversity and execution.

StudySetupFinding
Girotra et al. (2023)200 product ideas from MBA students vs. 200 from GPT-4, rated blind by consumersPurchase intent 40% → 49%; 35 of the top 40 ideas from GPT-4; human ideas slightly more novel

GenAI also erodes what a wise crowd needs: if everyone brainstorms with the same model, diversity and independence drop. Contests and communities must protect them. The crowd itself changes: with vibe coding nearly anyone can ship an app (new App Store apps rose about 30% in half a year), so the bottleneck shifts from building to attention, quality and demand. Amazon Mechanical Turk closes in 2026 because AI does simple micro-tasks; demand moves to scarce experts and real-world data.

Session 03 · 4 tasks · 14.5 P
Task 3.14 P

A bakery chain has developed three new sandwiches and wants its customers to predict which one will sell best before launching one nationwide. It plans an online poll. Pick two of the four wisdom-of-crowds conditions and name, for each, one specific design choice that ensures the poll delivers a good prediction.

Model answer
  • Diversity of opinion: many different people with their own information. Invite customers from all regions, store types (station, city centre, rural) and age groups, not only staff or the most loyal app users.
  • Independence: opinions are not shaped by others. Everyone votes privately; no running count, comments or "staff favourite" before the poll closes.
  • Decentralization: people draw on local knowledge. Run the poll in every store region, so commuters judge breakfast options and city-centre customers judge lunch, instead of one tasting panel at head office.
  • Aggregation: a mechanism turns private judgments into one result. Fix in advance that all votes across all regions are counted and the sandwich with the most votes is launched.

Task 3.44 P

The head of innovation at a consumer goods company says: "Generative AI makes our crowdsourcing obsolete. It writes a hundred product ideas in minutes for almost nothing." Give two arguments for and two against, each backed by a research finding or example from the lecture.

Model answer
  • For: AI ideas are better on average. In Girotra et al., consumers rated GPT-4 ideas with higher purchase intent than MBA students' ideas (about 49% vs. 40%), and 35 of the top 40 came from GPT-4.
  • For: AI is far faster and cheaper. In Boussioux et al., AI solutions were rated more valuable than the crowd's and cost about 27 seconds and USD 0.04 each.

4.1

Six tools to find innovation opportunities

Core idea

Don't look at your product; look at what the customer wants to achieve with it. All six tools shift the view from the offer to the customer's value-in-use.

The customer system separates three roles. The buyer places the order and pays; you always know them. The user gains the value and may be unknown to you. The influencer shapes the decision of buyer, user or both and is often invisible. Each has different needs, which the lecture shows with hearing aids.

Constraints on consumption show who is locked out today: skills (photography in the 19th century, computing in the 1970s), wealth (air travel before the 1970s), access (telephony before mobiles, niche films before Netflix) and time (trading collectibles before eBay). Removing one opens a new market.

4.2

Internal vs. external diffusion

Core idea

An idea must first win inside the firm, then in the market. Inside, promoters remove barriers; outside, it spreads along an S-curve and must cross the chasm to the mainstream.

Example

Lisa's AI agent. A procurement specialist builds a working AI agent that reads supplier mails. She is the product champion; the barrier is lack of willingness, so the missing role is a management champion. Bottom-up diffusion like this is common: 75% of knowledge workers already use GenAI at work.

Diffusion is getting faster: GenAI spread faster than the PC and the internet. But the better technology does not automatically win. August Dvorak's 1932 keyboard is faster, yet QWERTY, designed in 1868 so typewriter bars would not jam, is still the standard: habits, training and installed base keep it in place.

Don't confuse

Adoption is individual: one person's process from first hearing about an innovation to using it. Diffusion is a group phenomenon: the spread across a population over time, the sum of many adoption decisions (e.g. 30% of households).

4.3

How individuals decide to adopt (Rogers)

Core idea

Each person moves through five stages. Whether they adopt depends mostly on five perceived characteristics of the innovation, shaped by channels, social system, personality and nudges.

Exam-style tasks

Session 04 · 2 tasks · 6.5 P
Task 4.13.5 P2025 exam

Define diffusion and adoption and give one example for each.

Model answer

Adoption is the individual process from first learning about an innovation to deciding to use it and using it. Example: a craftsman who tries a tablet app for quotes and then uses it for every job.

Diffusion is the spread of an innovation across a population over time, the sum of many adoption decisions; it typically follows an S-curve. Example: the share of craft businesses in Germany that write quotes digitally.

A full answer has both definitions, the individual-versus-group distinction and one fitting example each.

5.1

Technology and big social and economic change

Core idea

Each big information technology made handling information cheaper and changed society. Such revolutions unfold over decades: installation, a turning point, then deployment.

The productivity J-curve explains why gains come late: firms first have to invest in complements such as skills, processes and organisation, so measured productivity dips before it rises.

5.2

How digital technology enables innovation

Core idea

Digital technology can be changed by code, built on by others and combined across devices. How much it enables depends on how restrictive it is (specificity) and how many actors it connects (relationality).

5.3

Why digital services dominate

Core idea

Digital services deploy instantly, scale at almost no marginal cost and learn fast. Each wave removed a constraint and moved control from incumbents to new entrants.

5.4

Tools for technology outlook and analysis

Core idea

Look out (outlook), look at your own firm (analysis), then decide when to act (alignment). Every tool has blind spots, above all for architectural innovation.

Limits

  • Systematic analysis prevents ad-hoc mistakes but can suppress dissent, become a proxy battleground and create false security.

6.1

Business model: where it comes from, what it is, what it's for

Core idea

A business model shows how a firm creates and captures value. It translates technology into economic output and can itself be a source of competitive advantage.

The term first appeared in 1965 and spread from the mid-1990s, driven by the internet and the "new economy", emerging markets and technology-dependent industries. Chesbrough & Rosenbloom (Berkeley) describe it as a framework that takes technological potential as input and converts it, through customers and markets, into economic output.

Firms use business models in three ways: to explain the business (Adobe explaining its switch to Creative Cloud subscriptions), to run it (UPS routing with ORION) and to develop it strategically (Snowflake adding a Data Marketplace).

6.2

Impact of servitization and digitalization

Core idea

Don't digitize what you are doing; digitalize what customers need done. As-a-service models are the result: the customer stops owning, the provider earns recurring revenue and sees usage.

6.3

Business model stages and hypothesis-driven BMI

Core idea

A business model ages from searching to growing to optimizing, and each stage speaks a different management language. New models are built by testing the riskiest assumption first.

6.4

Impact of data and analytics

Core idea

Data can improve processes, enrich an existing offer or become a new service. The further a provider goes from data to insight to action, the larger the joint sphere of co-creation.

7.1

What drives the need for transformation

Core idea

The environment changes continuously, but firms change in small steps. The gap grows into strategic drift until only a radical transformation helps.

The gradualist paradigm assumes a firm stays aligned through continuous small adjustments. In practice punctuated equilibrium dominates. The main driver is outside: new technology, new competitors, new customer habits such as GenAI. Only a minority of "learning organisations" adapt continuously, and less than 30% of transformation efforts succeed (McKinsey 2018).

8.1

Digital platform and the four roles

Core idea

A digital platform is a set of digital resources that enables value-creating interactions between external producers and consumers. Four roles: owner, provider, producer, consumer.

A digital platform is built on digital infrastructures such as the internet, smartphones and GPS. Three things make it digital: a networked, layered architecture; independence from product and hardware location; and functions that are not fixed in advance. Uber is the lecture's standard example.

8.2

Exchange vs. maker platforms

Core idea

Ask where the main value lies: in matching existing parties (exchange), or in what producers build on the platform (maker).

8.3

The four mechanisms

Core idea

Network effects and lock-in make a platform more valuable the bigger it gets and hard to leave. That pushes markets to one or two winners, while governance keeps behaviour in bounds.

I. Interaction
User groups keep control over the key terms of their interaction: price, bundling, delivery, ratings on marketplaces; chatting, liking, connecting on social networks.
II. Affiliation / lock-in
Users make platform-specific investments to take part: a fixed access fee (buying a console), resources (learning the iOS APIs), opportunity cost (a loyalty program). They would lose them by leaving, so switching is costly and the owner can set terms.
IIIa. Direct network effects
The platform gets more useful the more users of the same group join: WhatsApp, Signal, LinkedIn. Includes data network effects: more use improves the service (Google Maps traffic, Netflix recommendations).
IIIb. Indirect network effects
It gets more useful for one group as another group grows: more apps attract users, more users attract developers (iOS, PlayStation, ChatGPT apps); job ads and applicants.
IV. Governance
Mechanisms that bound and control behaviour without choking generativity: updates, control (gatekeeping, metrics, commissions), shared decision rights (moderators, peer review), incentives (YouTube Partner Program).
Winner takes all

A dominant design sets what users expect, and competition shifts to complementors. The innovator of a design need not win: Instagram copied BeReal (Candid, 2022) and CapCut (Edits, 2025).

Don't confuse

Direct: same side benefits (more friends on WhatsApp). Indirect: cross-side benefit (more listeners attract more artists). Lock-in is about what a user would lose by leaving, not about how many others are there.

8.4

Four business models for the digital era (Weill & Woerner)

Core idea

Position a firm by how much it knows about the end consumer and whether it works in a value chain or an ecosystem. Becoming an ecosystem driver is not the default best choice.

8.5

Power conflicts between owner and complementors

Core idea

The owner's governance power is the same tool that can harm complementors. The lecture gives no model solution; these cases apply the mechanisms above.

9.1

IoT and smart product capabilities

Core idea

Smart products sense, connect and act. Their capabilities build on each other: monitoring, then control, then optimization, then autonomy.

The Internet of Things is "the interconnection of machines and devices through the internet, enabling the creation of data that can yield analytical insights and support new operations" (Forbes Insights 2017). Related technologies are sensors, identification, connectivity, big data and IoT middleware.

A smart product is a physical product with sensors, a unique identity, connectivity, data storage, actuators and interfaces, so it can collect data and be reached and steered over the internet. The early IoT hype met disillusionment: no visionary push by an industry leader, offers that expected users to find their own needs instead of adding clear value, and the complexity of combining digital and physical offerings under one value proposition.

9.2

Value co-creation in smart service systems

Core idea

Value arises where the physical and digital worlds meet. Data travel up five layers, commands travel back down, and the smart product connects actors who never meet directly.

A smart service is the application of specialized competences through deeds, processes and performances enabled by smart products. In a smart service system, smart products are boundary objects that integrate the resources and activities of the actors for mutual benefit. When more actors interact with the product (third-party app providers, infrastructure providers, component suppliers), the system becomes an ecosystem.

AffordanceMeaningUnicorn Energy EnergyCube
IndividualActor-specific; depends on one actor's goals and capabilitiesMonitoring the battery's state of health
SharedSeveral actors share awareness of the same affordance (elevator status for owner, maintainer, tenants)Trusting the battery is available in a power outage
CollectiveFormed by integrating individual affordances; needs inter-organizational coordinationRecurring payment for a warranty extension based on battery condition

One smart product thus carries value propositions for several actors: the lead customer (battery status, UPS capacity), the service operator (remote fault detection), the technology provider (life-cycle insight) and the financial service provider (asset forecasting). Under the EU Data Act (from 12 Sept 2025) users decide who gets their product data. Connected cars show seven business model types for smart services (Sterk et al. 2024).

9.3

Impact on service innovation in incumbent firms

Core idea

Smart services give faster feedback and continuous improvement, but they force hardware, software and service teams out of their silos and into one project with many roles.

Tools and cases. The Smart Service Canvas (Pöppelbuß & Durst) checks the customer perspective (routines, jobs, pains, gains), the value perspective (smart product, smart service, revenue model) and the capability and ecosystem perspective (data, analytics, infrastructure). Heidelberger Druckmaschinen went from eCall and remote service on connected presses to a pay-per-outcome subscription that bundles machine, software, consumables and service.
